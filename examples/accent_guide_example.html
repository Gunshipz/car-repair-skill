2010 Hyundai Accent · 1.6 L · M5CF1 5-speed manual

Accent Shift Guide

Symptom: popped out of gear on the highway, and now the shifter won't go into any gear. The clutch master and slave are new and the clutch seems to work. This page walks the tests in order, cheapest first, shows where each part is, and lists what each result means and what it costs to fix.

The one question this page answers

Is the problem outside the transmission (the two shift cables, their end bushings, the cable bracket, the shifter, or an engine mount) or inside it (a shift fork, detent spring or interlock)? Outside fixes run $25–$230. Inside means a used transmission, $180–$600 DIY (Utah self-pull yards start at $135) or about $900–$1,650 at a shop. Test 4 settles it in about 20 minutes, with no parts.

Safety

For the roll test, the car is in neutral on flat ground with the parking brake off, so have someone in the driver's seat with a foot on the brake. Anything under the car goes on jack stands, never on just a jack. Engine off for everything except where a step says otherwise.

Questions for the owner

Answers to these make the research targeted: the right part numbers, the right donor transmission, and fewer wasted tests. Most take a minute at the car. Photos are better than descriptions.

The car itself

  1. The VIN (17 characters, on the dash at the windshield's lower corner or on the door-jamb sticker). It decodes trim and engine, and lets a dealer match exact part numbers.
  2. A photo of the driver's door-jamb sticker. The build month decides between two different factory transmissions: 43000-23017 (built through January 2010) and 43000-32818 (built from June 2010). Their shift forks and other internal parts differ.
  3. Trim: GS, GLS, SE or Blue? Used-transmission sellers file the Blue model's manual in a separate group.
  4. Mileage, and whether he knows it's the original transmission and clutch.
  5. A photo of the transmission's ID tag or casting stamp (on top of the case near the cable levers, once the air box is off). It says M5CF1 or M5CF1-1 and the final-drive ratio, which is what a yard needs to match a donor.

What happened

  1. Which gear was it in when it popped out, and at what speed? Accelerating, cruising, or letting off the gas? A pop-out under load points one way; one while coasting points another.
  2. Any noise at that moment: a bang, a clunk, grinding, or a whine that had been building?
  3. Any warning before: popping out of that gear before, a sloppy or vibrating stick, grinding into any gear, a clunk when getting on or off the gas (a clunk suggests a bad mount)?
  4. Since then: what does the stick do with the engine off? Does it move side to side? Forward and back? Locked solid, or floppy? This is test 1, and the single most useful answer.
  5. Does the car roll freely in neutral? (Test 2.)
  6. Has anyone forced the stick, or worked on it since?

History

  1. Why and when were the clutch master and slave replaced? What was the symptom then? Who did it, and was the line bled? Did the shifting feel normal afterward?
  2. Has the gear oil ever been changed, or checked? Any oil drips under the transmission?
  3. Any other work near the shifter or transmission: starter, mounts, axles, center console, or an accident?
  4. Has the shifter felt loose or vague for a while? Worn cable bushings show up first as slop.

Each answer narrows the search. The build date and VIN fix the part numbers. The engine-off stick behavior and the roll test split cable problems from internal ones. The pop-out details point to one fork or synchro.

How the shifting works

The gear stick isn't connected to the transmission by a rod. Two steel cables run from the base of the stick, through the firewall, to two small levers on top of the transmission:

  • The select cable moves when the stick goes side to side. It picks the column: 1-2, 3-4, or 5-R.
  • The shift cable moves when the stick goes forward and back. It pushes the chosen gear in.

Inside the transmission, those levers turn a control shaft, and its finger slides one of three shift rails. Each rail carries a fork that slides a gear's locking collar into place. Two small safety parts matter here:

  • A spring-loaded poppet (detent) ball clicks each rail into place, so a gear stays in. A broken poppet spring lets a gear pop out.
  • An interlock stops two rails from moving at once. If one rail is stuck partway into a gear, the interlock holds every other rail in place, and the stick feels locked in every direction. That fits "popped out of 5th, now nothing will go in."

Hyundai's own troubleshooting table for this transmission lists, under "Jumps out of gear": worn gear shift fork or broken poppet spring, and synchronizer hub-to-sleeve spline clearance too large. Both are inside the case. Outside causes that forums report on these cars are worn cable-end bushings, a cable that seized, a loose cable bracket, and a broken engine mount letting the whole engine rock and pull the cable.

The tests

  1. Shift with the engine off

    5 minutes · no tools

    Engine off, key out. Push the clutch to the floor and try every gear. Then do it again with the clutch pedal up. A clutch only matters when the engine is spinning, so if the stick still won't go anywhere with the engine off, the clutch is ruled out entirely.

    Pay attention to which way the stick is blocked. That tells you which cable or rail to suspect:

    side to side OK, won't go forward/backColumns can be selected but no gear goes in. Suspect the shift cable, or a rail stuck partway into a gear inside, which locks the others. won't move side to sideSuspect the select cable or the select side of the control shaft. locked solid every wayA cable is seized or jammed, or the transmission is caught between gears. Test 2 and test 4 decide. flops with no resistanceA cable end has come off its lever, usually a crumbled bushing. Go straight to test 5.

  2. Roll test

    2 minutes · a helper

    Flat ground. Stick in the neutral position (or as close as it goes), parking brake off, helper's foot ready on the brake. Push the car a few feet.

    rolls freelyThe transmission is in neutral. won't rollIt's actually in a gear even though the stick isn't. Something inside is holding a gear engaged, or the linkage is out of place. Test 4 tells which.

  3. Watch the levers on top of the transmission

    20 minutes · 10/12 mm sockets · a helper

    The transmission is on the driver's side of the engine bay. Its top is buried under the air box, the battery and the battery tray. Remove the air box (unplug the airflow sensor), then the battery and tray, to see the two cable ends. The factory manual removes these same parts to reach the cables.

    1. Cable end 1, with its accordion rubber boot.
    2. Its snap pin (a bent spring clip through the pin) holding it on lever 1.
    3. Cable end 2, also with a boot.
    4. Where it pins onto lever 2.
    5. The cable bracket bolted to the case, which holds the cable housings still.

    The factory manual doesn't say which lever is select and which is shift. Test 4 shows you: the select lever moves when the stick goes side to side.

    Have the helper move the stick while you watch both levers.

    a cable moves, its lever doesn'tThat cable end is slipping or off. Test 5. a cable housing moves tooThe bracket or a cable anchor is loose. Test 5. nothing moves at allEither the cable or the transmission is jammed. Test 4.

  4. Unclip both cables and move the levers by hand

    15 minutes · pliers, paint pen or phone camera

    First, mark the levers. Take a photo and draw a paint-pen line from each lever onto the case. One Accent owner on the Hyundai forum unclipped these to change a starter and lost reverse because a lever moved. Then pull the two snap pins and lift both cable ends off their levers.

    Now move each lever by hand. One lever picks the column, the other pushes the gear in. Work them together: pick a column, then push the gear lever each way. A gear is in when the car won't roll (helper tries pushing, clutch pedal up).

    levers move, gears engageThe transmission's shifting works. The fault is outside: cables, bushings, bracket or shifter. Tests 5–7. Cheap. levers move, no gear ever engagesInside fault. A broken fork or rail, or the control shaft has let go of the rails. levers won't move, or barelyInside fault. A rail stuck partway into a gear, a bent fork, or broken parts jammed in the case. Test 8, then the swap plan.

    Don't force the levers with a long bar. If they don't move with firm hand pressure, that is the answer.

  5. Check the cables, bushings and bracket

    15 minutes

    With both ends still off:

    • Push and pull each cable by its end. It should slide smoothly with two fingers. A stiff or crunchy cable is seized inside its housing, and the fix is a new cable. In one YouTube video, one cable felt fine and the other took real effort to move, and that stiff cable was the whole fault.
    • Look inside each cable-end eye. A rubber or plastic bushing should fill the hole snugly. Cracked, missing, or an eye that wobbles on the pin means replace the bushings.
    • Check the cable bracket. Grab each cable housing where it clips into the bracket and try to move it. It shouldn't shift at all. Snug every bolt on the plate. The factory torque for the cable-bracket bolts is 20–27 N·m (15–20 lb-ft).

  6. Check the shifter base under the console

    30–45 minutes · Phillips, 10/12 mm

    Only needed if tests 4 and 5 point outside and the cables look fine at the transmission end. Remove the center console (cup holder and parking-brake cover, side covers, then a front and a rear bolt). The two cables clip onto the bottom of the stick: the select cable with a snap pin, and the shift cable with a U-clip.

    Look for a missing snap pin or clip, a cable end that's popped off its pin, a cracked plastic base, and an adjuster collar that has slipped. A forum owner with a new cable had the plastic adjuster collar slip out going into 5th/reverse.

    Putting the shifter back: fit the cables with both the stick and the transmission levers in neutral, then bolt the shift lever assembly down to 9–14 N·m (6–9.5 lb-ft) and set the select-cable adjuster with the stick in N before the console goes back on (factory procedure).

    Hyundai's adjustment procedure comes from service bulletin 06-40-011, "Manual Transaxle Shift Lever Cable Adjustment", found in Chilton Library through the library card. It was written for the 2006 Accent; the 2010 uses the same cable and adjuster, so it should apply, but that's my inference. With the console off:

    1. Slide the adjuster's white cover rearward.
    2. Push locking tab K down with a small flat screwdriver. That frees cable L to slide in the adjuster.
    3. Move the gear stick fully to the right. That pulls adjuster M forward to its set point.
    4. Push tab N to lock it.
    5. Check that the stick moves left and right evenly in neutral. If not, repeat steps 2–4.
    6. Slide the white cover back. Hyundai's labor allowance: 0.4 h.

    The adjuster sits on the select cable per the factory manual, so in photo 2 the lower cable is probably the select cable. That's my reading, not labeled in the source.

  7. Check the engine and transmission mounts

    20 minutes · pry bar, flashlight, a helper

    Four rubber mounts hold the engine and transmission. If one tears, the whole engine rocks under load, which can pull a gear out and stretch the cables. That is one of the classic "pops out of gear" causes. Inspect each one for torn rubber and gaps. Then, with the hood up and the shifting fixed, have a helper hold the brake, put it in 1st, and ease the clutch out slightly while you watch from the side. The engine should only twitch.

    Mount bolts, factory: main through-bolts on the engine and transmission mounts 68.6–93.2 N·m; bracket and roll-stopper bolts 49–63.7 N·m (that one is from Hyundai service bulletin 09-SS-005).

  8. Check the gear oil and the drain plug magnet

    20 minutes · 17 mm or 19/24 mm (videos disagree) · drain pan · jack stands

    Low oil is the main thing that kills these transmissions, and the drain plug's magnet shows how much metal is loose inside. Pull the fill plug first (if the fill plug won't come out, you don't want the drain plug out). Oil should be right at the bottom edge of the hole.

    full, clean, fine paste on magnetNormal wear. low or emptyFind the leak. Low oil can make the rails bind. chunks, slivers, tooth piecesSomething broke inside. Plan the swap.

    Spec: SAE 75W/85 API GL-4, 1.9 L (2.0 qt). Plugs 60–80 N·m with new washers. Avoid GL-5 gear oil; it's meant for differentials and is rough on brass synchronizers.

If it's inside the transmission

Rebuilding an M5CF1 is a specialty job: the factory overhaul time is 12.2 h, and US rebuild kits for this exact gearbox are hard to find. A used unit is the normal fix. The factory time to swap one is 4.9 h. The subframe has to come down, and the engine is held from above with a support bar (Hyundai tool 09200-38001, or a rented engine support bar).

One in-car look that may be possible: on the Rio and Accent models before and after this one, Hyundai's manual unbolts the control shaft (part 1) from the top of the case with the transmission in the car, then reinstalls it in neutral with sealant. If the MC works the same way, you can look at the shift finger and the top of the rails without pulling the transmission. That's a hypothesis: the 2010 manual doesn't describe it. The factory torque for those bolts is listed as both 20–27 and 30–35 N·m in different places.

Buying a used transmission
  • Read the build date on the driver's door-jamb sticker. Hyundai's 2010 Accent transmissions are 43000-23017 (built 11/2006–01/2010) and 43000-32818 (built 06/2010–01/2011).
  • Check the trim. car-part.com files the "Blue" trim's manual in its own interchange group, separate from GS/GLS/SE. Blue units are cheap, but buy from the group that matches the car.
  • Kia Rio units: same gearbox family, but car-part.com's interchange lists Rio transmissions only for Rios. Only buy one if the seller guarantees Accent fit.
  • Ask for mileage, a test note ("run and drive"), and the warranty term. Shipped units come drained.

Do while it's out

The clutch, flywheel and release bearing come out with the transmission anyway, so the extra cost is parts only. A clutch kit is $80–$145. Also replace the axle seals and input seal ($3–$9 each), new cable-end bushings, any torn mount, and fresh gear oil.

Appendix A · Is this common, and what did others do?

Short answer: uncommon, and not a known weak point

On 2006–2011 Accents, Rios and Souls (all the same gearbox family), not being able to get into gear and popping out of gear are both rare in the records. There's no recall and no Hyundai bulletin for either. When "won't go into gear" does happen, the best-documented cause is a shift-cable end or linkage that came apart. That's my judgment from the numbers below.

Complaints undercount gearbox faults, because people file safety complaints for air bags and engines more often. Manual cars are also a minority of these models. Even allowing for both, the order of magnitude says rare.

What fixed it, when owners said

  1. Cable or linkage end: 2 firm fixes and 2 partial. It usually shows as a sudden loss of reverse, 1st or 5th, or a stick with no feel. Example: a 2008 Accent at 170k miles lost reverse, 1st and 5th; "the linkage had come apart."
  2. Clutch hydraulics: 1 firm fix. A 2009 Accent went into gear with the engine off but not running; a new master, slave and line fixed it. That doesn't match the owner's car, which won't go in even with the engine off.
  3. Shifter lower skirt: 1 firm fix, on the 2010 Soul only (Kia bulletin about entering 1st at a stop).
  4. Inside the gearbox (fork, detent spring, synchro): no poster-stated fix. These threads ended with a mechanic's guess or a replacement gearbox. A 2000 Accent (an older gearbox) had new cable bushings that didn't help, then got a used transmission.

Which causes fit the owner's car

The key fact: it won't go into gear even with the engine off. That rules out the clutch, since a clutch matters only while the engine spins. It leaves the cable ends, the shifter base, or the inside of the gearbox. Two causes explain both halves of the symptom, popping out and then no gear:

  • A cable end that let go (cheap, test 3–5). A slipping cable can drag the lever toward neutral under load, and once off, nothing engages. One forum owner watched the stick push itself out of 4th as the bushings wore.
  • A broken fork, rail or detent inside (expensive, Appendix C). This is the factory table's classic pop-out cause; if a rail jams partway, the interlock locks the others.

A bad engine mount can cause the pop-out but wouldn't leave every gear dead afterward. Test 4 separates the two remaining causes.

Reports most like the owner's

  • 2009 Accent, 107k: popped out of 5th, then 4th, then "will not go into or out of any gear, shift is stuck." No resolution posted. StartMyCar
  • 2009 Accent, 49k: popped out of 4th and 5th on the highway, got worse until it wouldn't hold 5th. Two dealers couldn't reproduce it. (NHTSA 10420100, CarTalk)
  • 2011 Accent: stuck in 4th, the stick had "no back pressure," cables attached and moving. The owner suspected the inside of the gearbox; unresolved.
  • 2008 Accent, 170k: lost reverse, 1st and 5th; the linkage had come apart. Fixed by reconnecting it.
  • 2007 Accent hatchbacks: a broken cable or cable end; one replacement cable end kept slipping until glued.

Appendix B · Pulling the transmission and replacing the clutch

The clutch sits between the engine and the transmission, so the two jobs go together: the transmission has to move away from the engine before the clutch can be reached. Every torque below is Hyundai's factory value (charm.li service manual, or Hyundai bulletin 09-SS-005 in Chilton) unless marked otherwise. Full write-up with a source for every number: rr_clutch.md in the research folder.

Two ways to do it

A. Factory: subframe out. The whole front subframe (the steel cradle that holds the lower arms, steering rack and engine mounts) comes out, and the transmission drops straight down. Hyundai's bulletin 09-SS-005 lists it as 42 photographed steps. It's built for a lift and heavy work on jack stands, and it disturbs the steering.

B. Subframe stays. Unbolt the transmission and slide it toward the driver's wheel well far enough to swap the clutch. A forum owner did this on this exact gearbox, and a 2007 Accent video shows it. The transmission can't come fully out this way; the video's narrator says so at 23:05. One forum poster did get a transmission out by removing the clutch first for room, then swinging the transmission out.

My judgment: route B for a clutch-only job, since there's no subframe, steering joint or power-steering hose to deal with. Route A when the transmission must leave the car (a used-unit swap or a rebuild), unless the clutch-first trick works.

Tools and supplies

  • Jack stands (four is safer), floor jack, wheel chocks. Lift points are the rocker seams behind the front wheels and ahead of the rear wheels (figure below).
  • Engine support bar across the fender lips, hooked to the engine (Hyundai tool 09200-38001), plus a second jack with a wood block under the oil pan as backup. Often a free loaner at AutoZone and O'Reilly; call first.
  • Transmission jack, or a floor jack with a cradle and a ratchet strap.
  • Clutch alignment tool (usually in the clutch kit), ball-joint/tie-rod separator, pry bars, torque wrench (10–100 N·m) plus a breaker bar for the 200–260 N·m axle nut.
  • Sockets 10, 12, 14, 17, 19 mm, plus 24 mm for the drain plug (sizes come from videos and disagree; test-fit). Universal joint and long extensions for the buried starter and cable-bracket bolts.
  • Parts: clutch kit (disc 215 × 145 mm, pressure plate, release bearing), two new axle circlips, two axle seals, cotter pins, 2 qt of 75W-85 GL-4 gear oil, moly grease (Hyundai CASMOLY L9508 or equal), DOT 3/4 brake fluid. Optional: slave cylinder ($12–$39) and flywheel resurface ($40–$100) or new ($68).

Removal (both routes start the same)

  1. Battery out, negative first. Mark every connector and hose with tape.
  2. Air box, intake hose, battery tray. Unplug the airflow sensor and the engine-computer connectors on the air box. Rio video: 10 mm air-box bolts, 12 mm tray bolts.
  3. Ground cable (one is on a bellhousing bolt), speed sensor and back-up light connectors.
  4. Clutch tube brackets, then unbolt the slave cylinder and hang it with its hose still connected, so no bleeding is needed later.
  5. Shift cables: snap pins off both ends, then the cable bracket (count the bolts: the manual says 2, some sources say 3). Bag the rubber grommets.
  6. Starter (2 bolts). Hang it by a strap, cable attached.
  7. Front wheels off, under covers off. Drain the gear oil now, before the axles come out, or it pours out of the axle holes.
  8. Axles: loosen the axle nut (helper on the brake), free the knuckle (tie-rod end, ball joint or strut bolts), pry each axle's inner end out of the case without digging into the seal. Throw away the circlips.
  9. Engine support bar on, taking a little weight. Route B keeps the right engine mount connected.
  10. Mounts: left transmission mount bracket, then rear and front roll stoppers (route B: unbolt them from the transmission).
  11. Bellhousing bolts: about seven, two big ones on top. Paint-mark each bolt's hole; they differ in length.
  12. Route B: pull the transmission straight off the input shaft toward the wheel well (never let it hang on the shaft) and rest it on the subframe. Route A: steering joint (mark it first), power-steering return hose, tie rods, lower arms, sway-bar links, roll stoppers, subframe stays, then lower the subframe on a jack and lower the transmission out.

Clutch replacement

  1. Pull the release bearing and fork out of the bellhousing (fork b, bearing c in the photo below). The fork pivots on a ball stud called the fulcrum.
  2. Put the alignment tool in the disc so it can't drop. Loosen the pressure plate bolts in a star pattern, a turn or two at a time. The video's cover has 6 bolts and 3 dowels.
  3. Inspect: new disc thickness is 8.5 ± 0.3 mm. Check the pressure plate for heat marks, cracks and uneven spring fingers (limit 0.5 mm), the bearing for roughness, and the fork tips for wear. Don't wash the bearing or disc in solvent.
  4. Flywheel: resurface or replace it. The video and forums both recommend it. The factory pages give no runout or minimum-thickness spec for this flywheel, so let the machine shop measure it. Flywheel bolts are 117.7–127.5 N·m.
  5. Grease, thin films only (too much makes the clutch slip and shudder). Per the bulletin: fulcrum tip about 0.2 g, inside the bearing 0.5–1.0 g, each bearing clip 0.3–0.5 g, fork pivot groove 0.5–1.0 g, fork-to-slave contact 0.4–0.8 g, input splines about 0.2 g.
  6. Clean the flywheel and pressure plate faces with brake cleaner. Fit the disc the way its "flywheel side" marking says, center it with the tool, set the pressure plate on its dowels, then tighten in a star pattern to 15–22 N·m.
  7. New bearing on the fork, with the fork's tips hooked in the bearing's clips. If you replace the fulcrum: 55–60 N·m (factory torque table).
  8. Before closing up, refill the clutch reservoir (it's shared with the brakes) and pump the pedal to check the slave isn't leaking. The video caught a leaking slave this way.

Putting it back together

  1. Slide the transmission onto the input shaft straight, then pull it flush with the bolts, not by prying. Bellhousing bolts: upper 60–80 N·m, lower 43–55 N·m. Starter 27–34 N·m.
  2. Mounts: transmission mount bracket 70–93 N·m (the factory lists 70–95 in the transaxle table and 68.6–93.2 on the engine-removal page; 70–93 meets both), support bracket 60–80 N·m, roll stoppers 50–65 N·m. Take the support bar's weight off only after the mounts are snug.
  3. Route A: subframe 93.2–117.7 N·m, stays 44.1–53.9, sway-bar links 98.1–117.7, steering joint 88.3–107.9, tie-rod ends 22.6–33.3 N·m (this list is from service bulletin 09-SS-005; the manual's table prints 24–34 for the tie-rod ends). Then get an alignment check (my judgment).
  4. New axle seals (flush) and new circlips. Push each axle in until it clicks. Axle nut 200–260 N·m with a new cotter pin, ball joint 100–120, wheels 90–110 N·m.
  5. Gear oil: 75W-85 GL-4, 1.9 L, through the side fill hole until it runs out. Plugs 60–80 N·m.
  6. Cable bracket 20–27 N·m, cable ends pinned, then the cable adjustment from test 6. Slave cylinder 15–22 N·m. If the hydraulic line was opened, bleed at the slave: slow pedal strokes, close the bleeder with the pedal held down.
  7. Test: pedal firm with the engine off, every gear selects at a standstill, no slip in 3rd/4th, no shudder. Check for leaks at the axle seals and fill plug, then recheck the axle nuts and mounts after a few miles.

Time, and mistakes others made

Hyundai's shop times: transmission swap 4.9–5.2 h, clutch 5.3 h, on a lift. My estimate for a first-timer on jack stands, route B: 12–20 hours over two days. One forum owner who called himself "old and slow" took 5 days.

  • Draining the gear oil after the axles are out, which spills it from the axle holes.
  • Not supporting the engine: with two mounts off it will drop.
  • Losing the cable-bracket grommets.
  • Letting the transmission hang on the input shaft, which bends the disc.
  • Too much grease, which makes the clutch slip and shudder.
  • Reusing the axle circlips, or prying into the axle seal.
  • Skipping the flywheel resurface, or pairing a new disc with an old pressure plate.
  • Using GL-5 gear oil.
  • Forgetting the cable adjustment afterward.

Where the sources disagree: the drain plug torque is 60–80 N·m in the transmission table but 35–45 N·m on the axle page (use 60–80). Plug sizes are 17 mm, 15/16 in or 24 mm, depending on the video. The disc's rivet-depth limit is printed as 0.3, 1.1 or 2.1 mm, which doesn't matter with a new disc. Not found for this car: the flywheel runout spec, the axle-nut socket size and the bellhousing bolt lengths.

Appendix C · Rebuilding the transmission

Short answer: possible at home, but a used unit usually wins

The M5CF1 can be rebuilt on a bench with ordinary tools plus a hydraulic press, a bearing puller and a depth micrometer. The wrenching isn't the hard part. Pressing synchro hubs and bearings on straight, and shimming the bearing end play correctly, is. If the case-open inspection shows only a worn fork or a broken detent spring, a $230–$300 parts repair is attractive. If hubs, sleeves or bearings are worn too, parts approach the price of a new transmission, and a $135–$255 Utah self-pull unit wins.

What's inside, in plain words

Two parallel shafts carry pairs of gears that are always meshed. Each gear spins freely until a synchro sleeve slides over and locks it to the shaft. A brass-or-steel synchro ring first matches the speeds so it doesn't grind. A shift fork rides in a groove around each sleeve and is pinned to a sliding rail. Under each rail, a spring-loaded poppet (detent) ball clicks into notches to hold it in gear. The factory table lists three causes for jumping out of gear: a worn fork, a broken poppet spring, or too much play between a synchro hub and its sleeve. All three are inside the case.

The job, in order

  1. Remove the transmission (Appendix B). Drain it, clean the outside.
  2. Rear cover off, then the 5th-gear stack: unstake and remove the lock nut (36 mm thin-wall socket), pull the 5th hub, sleeve, ring and gear. Drive out the 5th fork's roll pin with a 3/16 in punch.
  3. Poppet bolts out (14 mm). Keep each spring and ball with its bore; the springs can differ in length.
  4. Split the case. Shift into 3rd to drive out the 3-4 fork pin, then 1st for the 1-2 pin. The rails and forks lift out as a set. Then the input and output shafts come out together.
  5. Inspect before buying anything (table below). This decides whether to rebuild or buy a used unit.
  6. Rebuild the shafts with the press: hubs and bearings on square, sleeves facing the right way (marks), keys seated.
  7. Reassemble: shafts, rails and forks with new pins, case sealant, then measure and set the bearing end play with spacers per the manual. Fit the poppet balls and springs with sealant on the bolts.
  8. Bench test: turn the input shaft and move the shift lever by hand into every gear. The interlock must stop reverse after 5th until you return to neutral.

D1 · Moving the car while it's stuck

Hyundai's towing rules: a flatbed is best. A wheel-lift tow is OK only with the front wheels off the ground and the transmission in neutral, for no more than 19 miles at no more than 30 mph. If it won't go into neutral, flatbed only. Never tow it with all four wheels down, because that requires a transmission "in correct working order" (bulletin 11-GI-007).

To get neutral by hand: air box and battery off, photograph and paint-mark both levers, pull the two snap pins and lift the cables off. Move the side-to-side lever to the middle (3rd/4th) column and the other lever to its center click. Confirm with the roll test. If a lever won't move at all, it's a flatbed.

D2 · Crumbled cable-end bushing

The end of a cable wobbles on its lever stud or has popped off, with bits of bushing around it. The cable can stay in the car: pick out the old bushing, press in the Torque Solution TS-SCB-210 kit (Delrin, fits 2000–2011 Accents, $25–$33), and snap the end back on. Reset neutral at both ends if the levers moved.

D3 · Cable seized, kinked or broken

With the ends unclipped, one cable won't slide by hand, or it's snapped. The two cables are sold as one assembly (43794-xxxx, the suffix depends on build date; Pioneer CA8124 $97.79). The factory procedure removes only the center console, not the dash: cabin end off the shifter, the plate on the firewall (2 nuts), the transmission end, then pull the pair into the engine bay. Factory time 4.0 h (+0.6 h with A/C). Owners of a related Rio say feeding cables through the firewall is the fiddly part, and to work one cable at a time. Install with both ends in neutral, then adjust (test 6).

D4 · Rusted control shaft at the transmission

With the cables off, a lever is stiff or gritty, there's rust where its shaft enters the case, and the rubber boot is torn. You can tell it from an internal failure because the lever still travels and clicks through its positions. A broken part inside usually lets it move freely without engaging, or stops it dead in one direction (my reading; no manual gives a test). The fix that worked on a related Kia: wire-brush, penetrating oil, work the lever back and forth, soak overnight, repeat. Then grease it and replace the boot (43127-32000, $3.77). Keep torch heat away from the aluminum case and the seal. If the lever moves freely but nothing engages, it's an internal problem: see Appendix C.

D5 · Bracket, threads, shifter base

  • Cable bracket loose or bent: re-tighten to 20–27 N·m, or swap in the old car's or a yard bracket. A stripped hole in the aluminum case takes a thread insert (Time-Sert or Heli-Coil). Measure the bolt first; M8 × 1.25 is likely but unconfirmed.
  • Shifter base cracked, or the select-lever pin broken: the parts are $2–$30 each from a Hyundai parts site, or about $40 for a whole shifter at a yard. 0.4–0.7 h with the console out.
  • Cable adjustment wrong after reassembly (missing gears, hard reverse): most often a transmission lever moved while the cable was off. Set both ends to neutral, then do the adjustment from test 6.

D6 · What the gear oil tells you

  • Full, amber, fine gray paste on the magnet: normal. A clean drain doesn't clear the forks and detent springs, which wear without shedding much metal.
  • Low or empty: find the leak (axle seals, input seal, control-shaft seal) and fix it while it's apart. Empty means the bearings ran dry, so treat the gearbox as suspect.
  • Burnt smell, blue-tinted parts: overheated. Swap the transmission.
  • Milky: water got in. Find the path; rust inside means swap.
  • Chips you can feel an edge on, broken teeth, a snap ring: broken internals. Swap to a used unit; don't rebuild by guessing. A loose fragment can also be what's jamming a rail.

D7 · At the clutch

  • Disc wet with engine oil (thin, smells like motor oil, wet behind the flywheel): the rear main seal is leaking. Replace it now, while the flywheel is off. OEM 21443-22000 (72 × 96 × 9 mm), aftermarket $3–$24 (ITM 1500303, Fel-Pro BS40672, SKF 28308). Pry the old one out without scratching the crank, then tap the new one in flush. Trap: RockAuto also lists Victor Reinz 815406910 for this car, but that's the later engine's seal. If the joint around the seal's housing is what's wet, the oil pan has to come off, a much bigger job.
  • Disc wet with gear oil (thick, sulfur smell): the transmission's input seal ($3.69–$8.49). Replace it.
  • Flywheel glazed or lightly scored: resurface it ($40–$100). Cracks, deep grooves, blue heat marks or damaged starter teeth: replace it ($68–$106).
  • Pressure plate fingers broken, bearing destroyed: the bearing was dragging. Fit a new kit, and check the slave pushrod and pedal free play so it doesn't repeat.
  • Release fork worn where it touches the bearing: $9–$35 at a yard, or $108 from Hyundai (41430-23200). New slave already leaking: replace it ($12–$40) and bleed.

D8 · Axles and differential

A broken axle or differential can feel like popping out of gear: the engine revs and the car doesn't move. But the stick still shifts normally, so it doesn't explain the owner's locked shifter. While the axles are out anyway, replace both axle seals (manual transmission: 43119-39020 right, 43119-39030 left, about $22 each; the cheaper Pioneer 759100 is tagged for automatics) and fit new circlips. The axle nut is 200–260 N·m and single-use. Reports of its socket size disagree, so bring 30, 32 and 34 mm.

D9 · Donor transmission traps

  • Manual or automatic? No yard list says, but NHTSA's VIN decoder often does (the donor list above was decoded that way). Look for three pedals, a 5-speed knob, and two thin shift cables plus a clutch slave on the transmission. An automatic has cooler lines and a dipstick.
  • "Blue" trim: the 2010 Accent Blue (3-door only) has taller gears. Its 4th is 0.886 against 1.031 standard, and its 5th 0.733 against 0.837. In a GLS that's about 12–14% lower RPM in 4th and 5th, sluggish on Utah grades. Avoid a Blue box unless the owner's car is a Blue. Bench check: turns of the input shaft per turn of the differential in 4th are about 4.18 standard and 3.59 Blue. A 2009 listed as "BLUE" can't be the Blue trim (it started in 2010), so that's probably the paint color.
  • Build-date split: every post-January-2010 complete transmission in Hyundai's catalog is tagged "Accent Blue". The standard box, 43000-23017, covers November 2006 to January 2010. My read: a 2006–2009 Accent is the right donor for a GLS or SE. A dealer parts counter can confirm from the owner's VIN.
  • Kia Rio donors: same gearbox number, ratios and forks, but the bolt-on brackets are unverified and interchange guides don't list them as swappable. Only if you can test-fit the brackets. Avoid 2010–2011 Rios.
  • Move over from the old box: the mount bracket, cable bracket and grommets, wiring bracket, back-up light switch and speed sensor (new O-rings). Fit new input and axle seals to the donor.
  • Test it before installing: check the oil and magnet, check the input shaft for play, then turn the input through every gear by hand (each should turn the differential smoothly). After 5th, reverse should need a deliberate push.
  • At the yard: it weighs about 77 lb. Budget 3–5 h. Bring metric sockets 10–19 mm, a breaker bar, axle-nut sockets 30/32/34 mm, a battery impact, pry bars, a pin punch, penetrating oil, a drain pan, and a jack or plank. Pulling the clutch first gives room to swing it out. Many yards ban torches.

D10 · Seized bolts (Utah road salt)

Expect stubborn subframe bolts, axle nuts, mount bolts, exhaust hangers, and steel bolts in the aluminum case. Soak with penetrating oil the day before and again an hour before; a 50/50 mix of ATF and acetone did best in one published test. Tap the head to crack the rust, and work bolts back and forth rather than with one steady pull. Gentle torch heat on the nut or boss helps, but keep it away from brake lines, rubber, fuel and CV boots. Break the axle nut loose with the wheel on the ground. For a snapped bolt, try a left-hand drill bit before an extractor. Stripped threads in the case take an insert (Time-Sert M10 × 1.25 kit $124.99).

D11 · Cheap to do while it's apart

Front axles (manual-transmission versions) about $125 a pair, both axle seals $44, input seal $7, outer tie-rod ends $15–$50, lower ball joints $14–$34, all four mounts $130–$228, clutch hose $6, gear oil $25–$40. That's about $370–$540 together, and each one avoids its own 2–4 hour job later.
